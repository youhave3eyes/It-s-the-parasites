Chapter 01 · The hijack

How a parasite steers a nervous system

Scientists have watched parasites take control of animal brains for decades. We'll start there, because it shows what's possible. Then we'll look at how it works in people.

Proof from nature

3 documented cases
Toxoplasma gondiiHost: rat

The rat that stops fearing cats

The science

Infected rats lose their inborn fear of cat urine, and some become drawn to it. The parasite forms cysts in brain regions that include the amygdala, the fear center.

In plain words

The parasite can only breed inside a cat. So it rewires the rat to walk toward the thing that eats it.

Horsehair wormHost: cricket

The cricket that jumps into water

The science

The worm changes which proteins are made in the cricket's brain. Infected crickets seek out water, and the adult worm swims out of the body.

In plain words

The worm can only breed in water. So it makes a land insect drown itself.

Ophiocordyceps fungusHost: ant

The ant that climbs to die

The science

This parasitic fungus spreads through the ant's muscles and drives it to climb a plant and bite down on a leaf. Then it kills the ant and grows out of its head.

In plain words

It drives the body like a car. Up high, it can rain spores onto the ants below.

The wiring they tap

The gut–brain axis
The science

The gut has its own nervous system, with around 500 million neurons. The vagus nerve links it to the brainstem, and about 80% of its fibers carry signals up from the gut. The gut also makes most of the body's serotonin.

What that means

Your gut is a second brain that is always talking to the first one. Most of that talk goes up, from belly to head.

Anything living in your gut gets a seat at that table.

Four ways they get into your head

Mechanisms in humans
01 · ChemicalLab evidence

They make your brain chemicals

The science

Toxoplasma carries two genes for tyrosine hydroxylase, the enzyme that starts dopamine production. Infected brain cells have been shown to release more dopamine.

In plain words

Dopamine is your reward and risk chemical. A parasite that can turn it up can change how bold or impulsive you feel.

02 · InflammationWell established

They set off alarms

The science

Infection releases cytokines, immune signals that reach the brain through the blood and the vagus nerve. They trigger "sickness behavior": fatigue, low mood, poor focus and appetite changes.

In plain words

Your immune system fights back, and the fight itself leaves you tired, foggy and down.

03 · PhysicalWell established

They move in

The science

Pork tapeworm larvae can settle in the brain as cysts. The WHO links this condition, neurocysticercosis, to about 30% of epilepsy cases where the parasite is common.

In plain words

A cyst in brain tissue presses on nerves. That can mean headaches, seizures and confusion.

04 · TheftWell established

They steal nerve fuel

The science

The fish tapeworm absorbs vitamin B12 in the intestine. Nerves need B12 to keep up myelin, their insulation. A shortage can cause numbness, tingling and memory problems.

In plain words

Your nerves are wires with insulation. Take away the B12 and the insulation wears thin.

Food that keeps you clear

Parasites need a way in. The lowest-risk foods don't give them one. They have a protective skin, they never touch soil or animals, or they're cooked hot enough to kill anything along for the ride.

The low-risk list

And why each one works
Natural wrapper

Fruit you peel

Banana · orange · mango · avocado

The peel is a sealed package. Wash it, peel it, and the fruit inside has never touched the outside world.

Off the ground

Tree & vine fruit

Apples · pears · grapes · stone fruit

They grow up off the ground, away from soil and animal waste, where most parasite eggs live. Wash before eating.

Too dry

Nuts & seeds

Almonds · walnuts · sunflower · hemp

They hold very little water. Parasite eggs and cysts need moisture to survive.

Heat kills

Cooked grains & beans

Rice · quinoa · lentils · black beans

Boiling kills parasites and their eggs. Anything cooked all the way through is clean.

Rinse it off

Washed greens & herbs

Kale · spinach · lettuce · cilantro

Low risk once washed well under running water. Soil and slugs are the danger, so rinse leaf by leaf.

The fine print

Clean doesn't mean zero

  • Cyclospora outbreaks have been traced to imported berries, lettuce, basil and cilantro.
  • Toxoplasma eggs from cat waste can live in garden soil and stick to vegetables.
  • Rat lungworm larvae left by slugs on produce can travel to the brain.
  • Wild watercress can carry liver flukes.
The fix

Four habits

  • Rinse produce under running water. Rub or scrub firm fruits and roots.
  • Peel when you can.
  • Wash your hands after gardening, handling pets or cleaning the litter box.
  • Skip wild watercress and anything a slug has crawled over.

Chapter 03 · Carrier food

Food that carries them in

Meat and fish are muscle, and muscle is where many parasites hide. Their larvae curl up inside the tissue and wait to be eaten. Most are invisible to the naked eye.

The kill zone

USDA safe internal temperatures

Use a meat thermometer in the thickest part. Color isn't a reliable test. 145°F = 63°C · 160°F = 71°C · 165°F = 74°C.

For raw fish −4°F
for 7 days

The FDA says freezing fish at −4°F (−20°C) for 7 days kills parasites before it's eaten raw. Most home freezers don't reliably get that cold, so buy fish labeled sushi-grade or previously frozen.

Lime juice in ceviche doesn't kill parasites. Freezing doesn't reliably kill the trichinella in bear and other northern wild game. Cook those all the way.
